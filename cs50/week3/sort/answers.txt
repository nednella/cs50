sort1 uses: BUBBLE

How do you know?:
time taken to sort increases exponentially with increasing sample size, and is clearly affected by the data type (e.g., sorted was the fastest for all
sample sizes, followed by reversed, followed by randomised). Bubble sort is able to leverage pre-sorted data, which is clear as the pre-sorted data
was orders of magnitudes faster than the reversed and randomised data sets.



sort2 uses: MERGE

How do you know?:
1. Comparing times recorded for random5000 vs sorted5000 vs reversed5000 (and the same for the 10k and 50k sample size), they are approximately equal
for the given sample size of each data type.

2. the time taken to perform a sort on each data type with different sample sizes (5k, 10k, 50k) all increase linearly

3. The above coincides with the fundamentals of the merge sort, where the organisation of the data itself plays no part in how effective the sort is,
rather the time taken to complete the sort is purely dependent on the code.



sort3 uses: SELECTION

How do you know?:
unaffected by the data type, performs equally at all sample sizes regardless of being reversed, randomised or pre-sorted. The code is unable to
leverage the fact that the data is already sorted, as is clear by comparing both sorted and reversed data sets to to randomised. The upper and lower bounds
for the running times are equal across all data sets.
